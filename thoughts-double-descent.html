Double descent, and what the peak is really telling you

Learning notes. [MONTH] 2026.
Back to Thoughts

The picture

Everyone has seen the plot. Test error against model size: it falls, then rises to a spike, then falls again, ending up lower than the classical sweet spot ever got. Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal named it double descent and showed it across a range of models (arXiv:1812.11118). The spike sits at the interpolation threshold, where the number of parameters \(p\) first equals the number of samples \(n\) – the smallest model that can fit the training data exactly. Left of it you are in the world every statistics course describes, with a bias-variance U-curve. Right of it you are somewhere those courses said you should never go, and things get better. I want to write down what I think is actually happening, because for a couple of years I carried around a mystical version of this and it was not useful.

The peak is a conditioning problem

Take the cleanest case: linear regression with \(p\) features and \(n\) samples, no explicit regularization, and when \(p > n\) take the minimum-\(\ell_2\)-norm solution among the infinitely many that interpolate. This is ridgeless least squares, and Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani worked out its exact asymptotic risk in the proportional regime where \(p/n \to \gamma\) (arXiv:1903.08560). The estimator is \(\hat{\beta} = X^{+} y\), and its variance involves the inverse of the sample covariance's nonzero eigenvalues. So the question “why is there a spike at \(\gamma = 1\)” becomes “what happens to the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of a random \(n \times p\) design when \(p \approx n\).” The answer is: it goes to zero. In the Marchenko-Pastur limit the spectrum of the sample covariance is supported on an interval whose lower edge is proportional to \((1 - \sqrt{\gamma})^2\), which vanishes exactly at \(\gamma = 1\). Near-zero eigenvalues get inverted, the variance blows up, and the risk spikes. That is the whole mechanism. It is not a deep statement about learning; it is the same near-singularity that makes you reach for a pseudo-inverse tolerance in any numerical linear algebra code. Then why does it come back down past \(\gamma = 1\)? Because the smallest eigenvalue recovers – with more features than samples, the design is far from square and its nonzero spectrum moves away from zero again. There is also a second effect worth stating separately: with many more parameters than constraints, the minimum-norm requirement has a lot of room to work in, and it can satisfy the interpolation constraints with a small-norm solution that spreads the fit over many directions instead of loading a few. The implicit regularization gets stronger as you add parameters, which is precisely the opposite of the classical intuition and, I think, the genuinely interesting part.

The fact that reframes the whole thing

Here is the result I wish were on the same slide as the picture, every time: With optimally tuned ridge regularization, the peak does not exist. The risk curve becomes monotone in \(\gamma\). Hastie et al. show this directly, and it is not a technicality – it says the spike is an artifact of using zero regularization at the one aspect ratio where zero regularization is catastrophic. So double descent is less “a surprising new phenomenon of overparametrized learning” and more “a diagnostic image of what happens when you leave a regularization parameter at zero.” Which is a considerably less mystical statement, and much more actionable: if you see a bump in your own model-size sweep, your first hypothesis should be that your regularization is not being tuned per model size, not that you have discovered something profound. The genuinely surprising residue, after you subtract the peak, is still surprising: interpolating estimators can be consistent, and past the threshold bigger keeps being better. Benign overfitting is real. It just is not the same claim as the bump.

Where my own work touched this

Our label-shift paper (arXiv:2212.00700) has the same shape of result, which is why I keep coming back to this literature. The setup: binary classification with Fisher's linear discriminant, where the two classes appear in the training data with different frequencies. The universal advice is to balance the classes – subsample the majority until the counts match. That advice is derived, almost always, in the underparametrized regime where \(n \gg p\). Redo the asymptotics in the overparametrized regime and there is a phase transition: in a region of the parameter space, the classifier trained on the imbalanced data beats the one trained on balanced-but-smaller data. The reason is not exotic. Balancing throws away samples. When you are already short on samples relative to dimension, discarding data to fix a distributional asymmetry can cost you more than the asymmetry does. And the second half of that result rhymes exactly with the ridge story above: the phase transition vanishes as regularization becomes strong. Same moral, different problem. A surprising regime-dependent effect, and adequate regularization eats it.

What I actually took away

  • Nearly every “paradox” of high-dimensional statistics is a paradox relative to an intuition built at \(n \gg p\). The fix is to redo the calculation in the regime you are in, not to invent new intuition.

  • If a phenomenon disappears under optimal regularization, it is a statement about your tuning, not about the world. Say so.

  • Practical advice derived under one aspect ratio should be labelled with that aspect ratio. “Balance your classes” is good advice with a footnote, and the footnote is load-bearing.

  • Random matrix theory is worth the investment. Nearly all of this reduces to knowing what happens to the spectrum of \(X^\top X\), and once you have that reflex the surprising results stop being surprising in a way that is satisfying rather than deflating. Back to Thoughts
