Currently Reading

A running list. No endorsement implied – these are just what is open in my tabs.

  • Song, Dhariwal, Chen, Sutskever, Consistency Models, arXiv:2303.01469. Still the cleanest statement of the “map any point on the trajectory back to its origin” idea.

  • Salimans and Ho, Progressive Distillation for Fast Sampling of Diffusion Models, arXiv:2202.00512. The halving argument is worth working through by hand once.

  • Hastie, Montanari, Rosset, Tibshirani, Surprises in High-Dimensional Ridgeless Least Squares Interpolation, arXiv:1903.08560. The paper that turned double descent from a picture into asymptotics.

  • Belkin, Hsu, Ma, Mandal, Reconciling modern machine learning practice and the bias-variance trade-off, arXiv:1812.11118.

  • Messenger and Bortz, Weak SINDy. The weak-form idea applied to sparse system identification, and the reference I send people to first.

  • Isola, Zhu, Zhou, Efros, Image-to-Image Translation with Conditional Adversarial Networks, arXiv:1611.07004, and Zhu et al., CycleGAN, arXiv:1703.10593. Older, but I reread these whenever I want to remember what a loss function is for.

A Few Things I Believe

Loosely held, and I have changed my mind on at least two of these before.

  • If a method has a hyperparameter you are not tuning, your experiment is measuring the hyperparameter, not the method.

  • Most “surprising” phenomena in high-dimensional statistics are surprising relative to an intuition built at \(n \gg p\), and stop being surprising once you redo the calculation in the right regime.

  • A proof that a method cannot work is worth several weeks of engineering time, and it is almost always cheaper to obtain.

  • Numerical differentiation is a last resort. If you find yourself estimating a fourth derivative from noisy samples, back up and reformulate.

  • Benchmarks measure what is easy to measure. The gap between that and what you care about is where most of the interesting work is.
