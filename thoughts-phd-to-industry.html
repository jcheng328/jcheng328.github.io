What changed when I left academia

Experience. [MONTH] 2026.
Back to Thoughts 
This one is opinion rather than mathematics, and it is drawn from one person's path out of one department into one company. Discount accordingly.

The unit of work changed

In graduate school the unit of work is the theorem. You can spend three weeks on a lemma, produce nothing anyone else can see, and have had a genuinely good three weeks – because the lemma either holds or it does not, and finding out is the job. Progress is measured in understanding, and understanding is allowed to take as long as it takes. The unit of work now is the shipped change. Something is different in production, and either a metric moved or it did not. That is a real adjustment, and I do not think it is the adjustment people warn you about. The warning is usually “industry is less intellectually deep,” which has not been my experience. The actual adjustment is that the feedback loop closes, sometimes within hours, and you have to develop taste for how much thinking a decision deserves before you make it. In a proof, more thinking is always better. In a system, past some point more thinking is just latency. I overshot in both directions during my first months. Some things I overanalyzed that I should have just tried, because trying was cheap and the experiment would answer the question faster than I could. Other things I tried that I should have thought about first, because the failure mode was not one an experiment would surface.

What transferred, and it was not what I expected

I assumed the technical content would transfer and the rest would not. It was closer to the reverse. Knowing what is impossible. This is the single most valuable thing the Ph.D. gave me and I did not see it coming. A meaningful fraction of proposed work is proposing to beat an information-theoretic limit, or to identify something from data that provably does not determine it, or to estimate a quantity at a rate nothing can achieve at that sample size. Recognizing this in the first hour of a discussion rather than the sixth week of implementation is worth an enormous amount, and it is a reflex you can only build by having spent years being told by proofs that you cannot have what you want. Tolerance for being stuck. Years of thinking hard about something with no visible progress turns out to be excellent training for debugging a system nobody understands. The emotional skill is identical: stay with the confusion, do not thrash, keep narrowing. Comfort with approximation. Applied mathematics is the discipline of knowing which term you are allowed to drop. That is most of engineering judgment. Writing. Badly underrated. A great deal of influence here flows through documents, and having written papers – where every sentence gets contested – is unusual preparation. What transferred less well than I hoped: the specific theorems. I have used the actual content of my thesis approximately never. The habits of mind, constantly.

What atrophied

Reading depth. In graduate school I would read one paper for a week, reconstruct every proof, and find the two errors. I read far more papers now and far more shallowly. I feel this loss genuinely, and starting these notes was a deliberate attempt to push back on it – writing something up forces the depth back in, because you cannot fake your way through an explanation. Long-horizon problems. The question that takes eight months has a hard time surviving contact with a planning cycle. This is a real cost, and I do not think it is fully compensated by anything. Some problems only yield to sustained attention and the structure here does not naturally protect it. You can carve out that protection, but you have to do it on purpose. Being the person who knows the most about a thing. At the end of a Ph.D. you are, briefly, the world expert on a very narrow topic. That is a strange and pleasant status and you do not have it here. You are surrounded by people who know things you do not, which is better for you and worse for your ego.

Things I would tell year-four me

Your scale intuitions are miscalibrated and you do not know it. Everything I had built ran on a laptop. Nothing here does. The set of ideas that work at small scale and fail at large scale is bigger than I expected, and – the part that surprised me more – the set of embarrassingly simple ideas that only work at large scale is also bigger than I expected. You cannot reason your way to these; you have to have been burned. Learn the tooling before you need it. Distributed training, profiling, the data stack. I treated all of this as plumbing beneath my job. It is the job. The gap between an idea and a measured result is almost entirely tooling, and if that gap is wide for you personally, you will have fewer ideas – not because you think less, but because you unconsciously stop having ideas that are expensive for you to test. The job market is a matching problem, not a ranking. It felt like a ranking at the time. It was not. Talk to people about what the work is actually like day to day, because the answer varies enormously between teams with identical job titles, and that variance dwarfs the variance between companies. Finish the papers. Whatever is at eighty percent when you leave will stay at eighty percent forever. I know this because of the ones I did not finish.

Would I do it again

Yes, and not because it was a credential. Five years of being made to be precise – of not being permitted to hand-wave, ever, because a proof does not accept hand-waving – changes how you think in a way I have not seen another process produce. It is expensive and slow and there are cheaper routes into this work. But I use the thing it built every day, and it is not the theorems. Back to Thoughts
